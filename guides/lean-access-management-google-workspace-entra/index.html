Lean access management: keep Google or Microsoft as your identity core

Sources checked 22 October 2026. 9 minute read.

The short answer

You do not need an enterprise identity provider to control access well at 10 to 200 people. Use the Google Workspace or Microsoft Entra account you already pay for as the one list of who works here, sign in to apps through it where your plan allows, and keep a short list of the apps that live outside it. The part most teams skip is proof: after you remove someone, read each app's user list again and keep the dated result.

Why the identity core you already have is enough

Every company on Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 already has a directory: one account per person, with a clear state of active or suspended. That is the record an auditor asks you to reconcile against, and it costs nothing extra.

Both can act as the sign-in for other apps. Google Workspace lets an admin set up a custom SAML app, and Entra lets you add enterprise applications for single sign-on and, where the app supports it, automatic provisioning.

What a dedicated identity platform adds is mostly breadth and automation for large estates: more prebuilt connectors, lifecycle workflows and policy engines. Okta's Essentials suite, which includes lifecycle management, is listed at $17 per user a month, billed annually, with a $1,500 annual minimum. At 100 people that is $20,400 a year before any app upgrades.[1],[5],[6],[8]

The SSO tax, and how to plan around it

Many SaaS vendors put single sign-on, and often SCIM provisioning, only on their higher plans. The public list at sso.tax tracks this: for example, it shows Figma at $12 per user a month on the base plan and $45 on the plan with SSO.

A lean team does not need to pay that upgrade for every app. Pay for SSO where the app holds sensitive data or many people use it. For the rest, accept that the app keeps its own login, list it, and make sure its user list is checked when someone leaves and at every access review.[9]

An app outside single sign-on keeps working after you suspend someone in Google or Microsoft. Suspending the identity is necessary, not sufficient.

The playbook, step by step

  1. Step 1. Make the directory the source of truth[3],[4],[7]

    Every person who works for you, including contractors with a work account, is in Google Workspace or Entra. Leavers are suspended there first, the same day, and signed out of active sessions: in Google, suspend the user and sign them out; in Entra, block sign-in and revoke sessions.

  2. Step 2. Route sign-in through it where it pays[1],[6]

    Connect your highest-risk apps (code, cloud, finance, customer data) to Google or Entra for single sign-on. Where the app supports SCIM on your plan, turn on provisioning so a suspension also removes the account.

  3. Step 3. Control what apps can read from Google[2]

    In the Google Admin console, API controls let you mark third-party apps as trusted, limited or blocked, and review requests for blocked apps. This keeps apps that sign in with Google from quietly reading Drive or Gmail.

  4. Step 4. Keep a short list of everything outside

    For each app that keeps its own login, write down the owner, the admin page, and how you remove someone. GitHub outside collaborators, deploy keys, cloud access keys and shared passwords belong on this list too, because they never pass through your directory.

    The offboarding checklist generator at /tools/offboarding-checklist (a free tool) builds this list per app, with each vendor's own instructions.

  5. Step 5. Remove, then read again[10]

    When someone leaves, remove them in each app on the list, then pull the app's user list again and confirm they are gone. Record the date. A removal nobody checked is the gap auditors find: terminated users who kept access account for about 12% of SOC exceptions in one benchmark of 193 reports.

  6. Step 6. Review every quarter, with evidence[10]

    Once a quarter, pull each in-scope system's user list, reconcile it against the directory, decide keep, revoke or change for every account, and sign it off. The free template at /tools/access-review-template has the columns auditors commonly ask for.

When an enterprise identity platform is the right call

If that is you, a platform like Okta earns its price. If it is not, the playbook above covers the same ground for a fraction of it.

  • You have a dedicated IT or identity team and hundreds of apps to provision automatically.
  • You need advanced policies: device trust, adaptive MFA, complex group rules across many directories.
  • Most of your apps already require an enterprise plan for other reasons, so the SSO upgrades are paid anyway.

Questions

Can Google Workspace replace Okta for a small company?
For many small companies, yes: Google Workspace can be the directory and the sign-in for apps through custom SAML apps. You give up some automation and policy depth that a dedicated platform adds.
What is the SSO tax?
The extra price SaaS vendors charge for single sign-on, usually by putting it on a higher plan. sso.tax keeps a public list with base and SSO prices.
Does suspending someone in Google remove them from every app?
No. It stops sign-in through Google. Apps with their own passwords, personal access tokens, deploy keys and cloud access keys keep working until you remove them in each app.
How do I prove a removal happened?
Read the app's user list again after removing the person and keep the dated result with your access review evidence. A ticket that says someone was asked is weaker evidence.
